## What inspired you to create this app?

I live next to Autrey Mill Nature Preserve in Johns Creek and I've walked its trails my whole life. A few years ago I started noticing that some stretches of the forest floor all look the same, just privet and English ivy, and the wildflowers I remembered weren't there. The preserve's own volunteer list names 14 invasive plants on the property. Staff and volunteers can't survey 46 acres every week, so patches get big before anyone sees them. Big patches are expensive to clear and small ones are cheap, so the whole game is finding them early. I thought every visitor with a phone could be a sentry. Point the camera at a plant, let the app check it on the device, and drop a pin that staff can actually use. When I described the idea to staff at the preserve they said it sounded like a good idea, so I built it. The part I care about most isn't the AI, it's the loop. Spot, verify, act, tell. A report only matters if somebody can rank it, treat it, and close it.

## What technical challenge did you face and how did you solve it?

The hard part was making the app honest. A plant classifier is easy to demo and easy to fool. I trained an EfficientNet model on 33,564 iNaturalist photos of 112 plant species (plus not-a-plant), including the 14 invasives documented at the preserve plus the native look-alikes they get confused with, like coral honeysuckle next to Japanese honeysuckle. If I'd split the photos randomly the test score would've been inflated, because one observer's near-duplicate shots would leak between train and test. So I split by observer. Then I calibrated the probabilities with temperature scaling on a validation set and picked the "confirmed invasive" threshold to hit 97% precision on validation, which gave 95.9% precision and 78.8% coverage on the held-out test set, split by observer. The scanner never names a species unless it clears that bar. Everything else goes to a "needs expert review" queue or asks the user to rescan with a specific reason, like too dark or move closer, based on sharpness, exposure and a saliency check on each frame. It also refuses to capture if a person is in the shot. The model runs on the phone with Core ML, so it works on the trail with no signal. The second challenge was ranking reports so staff see the right ones first. I wrote a transparent score that favors small, isolated patches near the creek, with a per species weight from the Georgia Forestry Commission's Dirty Dozen list, and unit tested it. Held-out accuracy on iNaturalist photos is not field accuracy, so I added a field test mode and measured [Z%] on [M] photos at the preserve with [a volunteer/staff member] checking each label. That gap is in the model card.
