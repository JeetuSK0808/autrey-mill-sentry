## What inspired you to create this app?

I live next to Autrey Mill Nature Preserve in Johns Creek and I've walked its trails my whole life. A few years ago I started noticing that some stretches of the forest floor all look the same, just privet and English ivy, and the wildflowers I remembered weren't there. The preserve's own volunteer list names 14 invasive plants on the property. Staff and volunteers can't survey 46 acres every week, so patches get big before anyone sees them. Big patches are expensive to clear and small ones are cheap, so the whole game is finding them early. I thought every visitor with a phone could be a sentry. Point the camera at a plant, let the app check it on the device, and drop a pin that staff can actually use. When I described the idea to staff at the preserve they said it sounded like a good idea, so I built it. The part I care about most isn't the AI, it's the loop. Spot, verify, act, tell. A report only matters if somebody can rank it, treat it, and close it.

## What technical challenge did you face and how did you solve it?

The hard part was making the app honest. A plant classifier is easy to demo and easy to fool. I trained two EfficientNet models on 62,041 iNaturalist photos of 186 plant species (plus not-a-plant): the 14 invasives documented at the preserve, 84 more from the Georgia invasive list, the native look-alikes they get confused with, like coral honeysuckle next to Japanese honeysuckle, and 74 common natives from within four kilometres of the preserve, because my first field test showed that a sweetgum or a Christmas fern the model had never been taught could come back as a weak invasive guess. If I'd split the photos randomly the test score would've been inflated, because one observer's near-duplicate shots would leak between train and test. So I split by observer. Then I calibrated the probabilities with temperature scaling on a validation set that also includes copies of the photos degraded like trail shots (dusk with sensor noise, backlight, dappled light, hand-shake blur, a small far-away subject), and picked the "confirmed invasive" threshold to hit 98% precision on validation, which gave 97.5% precision and 66.5% coverage on the held-out test set. Under those simulated trail conditions the two-model ensemble averages 83.0% top-1, where the first version got 71.5%, and on held-out photos of the preserve's common natives it now says "native, nothing to report" 95% of the time and flags 1.1% as possible invasives, down from 16.1%. The scanner never names a species unless it clears that bar. Everything else goes to a "needs expert review" queue or asks the user to rescan with a specific reason, like too dark or move closer, based on sharpness, exposure and a saliency check on each frame. It also refuses to capture if a person is in the shot. The model runs on the phone with Core ML, so it works on the trail with no signal. The second challenge was ranking reports so staff see the right ones first. I wrote a transparent score that favors small, isolated patches near the creek, with a per species weight from the Georgia Forestry Commission's Dirty Dozen list, and unit tested it. Held-out accuracy on iNaturalist photos is not field accuracy, so I added a field test mode and measured [Z%] on [M] photos at the preserve with [a volunteer/staff member] checking each label. That gap is in the model card.
